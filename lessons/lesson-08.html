How to Read and Pronounce the Dogchen

With Gochen, the top letter went silent and the base letter kept its own sound. Dogchen works differently: when a letter attaches underneath, it often changes the sound of the whole syllable into something new.

Reading a Few Examples

ཀྱ

Spelled out as "ka, ya, tak, cha" — meaning "ya attached beneath ka" produces the sound cha. Neither the plain "ka" sound nor the plain "ya" sound survives on its own — together, they become a new sound. The same shift continues down the list: ཁྱ becomes chha.

ཀྲ

Spelled out as "ka, ra, tak, tra" — meaning "ra attached beneath ka" produces the sound tra. This time, the two sounds blend into a cluster you can actually hear both halves of.

ཀླ

Spelled out as "ka, la, tak, la" — meaning "la attached beneath ka" produces the sound la. Here, the attached letter's own sound takes over completely, and the base letter's sound disappears.

Why the Three Types Behave Differently

Notice that all three examples used the same base letter (ཀ), but produced three different outcomes:

  • ཡ underneath → blends into a completely new sound (ka + ya → cha)
  • ར underneath → both sounds remain audible, forming a cluster (ka + ra → tra)
  • ལ underneath → the underneath letter's sound wins out (ka + la → la)

This is simply something to get used to by exposure rather than logic alone — each of the three "attachers" has its own consistent behavior, and once you've seen a few examples of each, the pattern becomes predictable.

One Letter, Two Roles: A Real Example

Back in the last lesson, we mentioned that a letter can appear in both the Gochen and Dogchen lists. Here's a real word that shows exactly that:

སྤྱ

Monkey

The letter པ here is doing double duty: it has ས stacked on top of it (a Gochen), and it has ཡ attached underneath it (a Dogchen) — both at the same time, in the very same word. This is a perfectly normal, valid combination, not a special exception.

What You Should Be Able to Do Now

Before moving on, you should be comfortable with:

  1. Writing and pronouncing all thirty base alphabets
  2. Writing and recognizing the six categories of compounded alphabets (the three Gochen and three Dogchen sets)
  3. Writing and pronouncing the four vowels

If any of these still feel shaky, it's worth another pass through the earlier lessons in this chapter before continuing — everything from here on builds directly on top of these building blocks.
