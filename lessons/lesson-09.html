Identifying སྔོན་འཇུག, མིང་གཞི, རྗེས་འཇུག, and ཡང་འཇུག

Think about how an English word like bag is put together: "b" and "g" are consonants, and "a" is the vowel sitting between them. Every English word needs at least one vowel to hold it together.

Dzongkha follows that same basic idea — but it goes one step further. In Dzongkha, it's not enough to know which letters are in a word. You also need to know what job each letter is doing, because a letter's position in the word tells you something important about it.

The Four Jobs a Letter Can Have

1. The Main Letter — མིང་གཞི (Mingzhi)

Every word needs one letter that actually drives its sound — the letter everything else is built around. This is called the Mingzhi. The name itself tells you what it does: མིང means "word," and གཞི means "foundation" — so Mingzhi literally means "the foundation of the word." It can be a single plain letter, or one of the compounded (Gochen/Dogchen) letters you already learned.

2. A Vowel — དབྱངས (Yaang)

A vowel mark can attach to the Mingzhi, just like the vowel marks you learned earlier. Not every word has one, but many do.

3. A Letter Before the Main Letter — སྔོན་འཇུག (Ngonjug)

Sometimes a letter comes before the Mingzhi. There are exactly five letters that can do this job:

ག · ད · བ · མ · འ

4. A Letter After the Main Letter — རྗེས་འཇུག (Jejug)

Sometimes a letter comes after the Mingzhi. There are exactly ten letters that can do this job:

ག · ང · ད · ན · བ · མ · འ · ར · ལ · ས

5. A Letter After That — ཡང་འཇུག (Yangjug)

Occasionally, one more letter comes after the Jejug letter. There are exactly two letters that can do this job:

ད · ས

Seeing All Five Roles in One Word

Here's a real word that uses every single role at once:

འཇིགས
ཇ Drives the word's sound མིང་གཞི (Mingzhi)
འ Comes before the main letter སྔོན་འཇུག (Ngonjug)
ག Comes after the main letter རྗེས་འཇུག (Jejug)
ས Comes at the very end ཡང་འཇུག (Yangjug)
ི The vowel sound "ee" དབྱངས (vowel)

Once you can pick out each of these five pieces in a word, you're no longer just looking at a cluster of letters — you're reading a small, organized structure where every piece has a reason for being there.
