About this blog

My name is Chencho, and I work as a public servant. By training, I'm a software developer — but my real passion has always been language, particularly Dzongkha and English. Over the years, I noticed something: many Bhutanese, both adults and children, struggle to learn Dzongkha, even though the same people are often quite comfortable in English. That gap got me thinking — what if English, the language so many of us already know well, could actually be the bridge back to Dzongkha? By putting the two languages side by side, contrasting and comparing how each one works, learning Dzongkha might feel less like starting from zero and more like recognizing a pattern you already know. It helps that the two aren't as far apart as they might seem. English and Dzongkha exist for the same fundamental reason — they're tools humans built to communicate with each other, shaped by the same mouths, the same breath, the same basic biology, regardless of where in the world we happen to live. Once you look past the different scripts and sounds, there's real structure in common. I want to be clear about what this blog is, and what it isn't. This is in no way meant to be a superior or "better" method of learning Dzongkha. Dzongkha already has a well-established, time-tested tradition of teaching — the monastic schools of Bhutan have shaped how the language has been passed down for generations, and that tradition deserves far more credit than any blog could claim. What this is, instead, is a small and imperfect effort — one concerned Bhutanese trying to do something about a language I care about, and a language that, like many national languages around the world, is genuinely at risk of fading. If it helps even a few people — a child struggling in school, someone in the diaspora trying to reconnect, or anyone curious about Bhutan — reading, hearing, and speaking a little more Dzongkha, that's enough.

A personal project to keep Dzongkha alive and accessible.
